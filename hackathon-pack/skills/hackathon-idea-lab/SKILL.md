# Evidence-led idea lab

Produce a build decision that can survive a skeptical judge. Preserve the user's chosen domain and team constraints; offer a pivot when evidence warrants it, without silently replacing the assignment.

Start from the actual event rubric, available build hours, team strengths, and accessible credentials. Treat sponsor eligibility and useful sponsor integration as separate questions. Do not multiply headline prizes into a forecast of winnings or assume prizes can be stacked. If dates or rules conflict, record the conflict and the authoritative source needed to resolve it.

Research workflows, not just categories. Name the user, triggering event, current workaround, concrete output, and what the system can actually do. A narrower workflow can be useful even in a crowded market; explain which existing feature it improves and why that difference matters.

For an open brainstorm, create a compact shortlist with different underlying workflows. For a specific user idea, start with that idea and its closest alternatives. Browse current primary sources for the problem, direct competitors, and required data. Record direct URLs, retrieval date, and the bounded claim supported. Separate:

- **Observed problem:** complaints, regulator findings, documented work, or actual workflow artifacts.
- **Adoption/payment evidence:** independently established use, customer behavior, or purchases. A vendor's price page is evidence of an offer, not verified willingness to pay.
- **Inference:** plausible user value or unmet gap inferred from the above.
- **Unvalidated:** remaining buyer, frequency, integration, or efficacy assumptions.

Search to falsify the exciting idea. Search its main action plus the user's occupation, then the nearest substitute, incumbent feature docs, marketplace listings, and adjacent workflows. Follow the workflow into the user's current system of record: a specialist scanner may look novel while the POS or accounting system already performs the proposed financial action. Compare the composition of existing tools as well as standalone rivals. A failed search supports only “not found in this bounded search.” Never conclude that nobody has built it. Distinguish a shipping product, preview, research prototype, and vendor marketing claim. Include the strongest disqualifying discovery, not just weaker competitors.

For finalists, make the decision concrete: data available now; credentials still missing; one end-to-end weekend slice; a visible result; one surprising recovery or refusal; a held-out judge scenario; success and kill criteria. Label real, cached, synthetic, and simulated components. Do not claim a tested financial/safety outcome from a passing software fixture.

Rank with disclosed judgment rather than invented precision. Use a hard gate for inaccessible core data or an unbuildable critical path. Show whether a different reasonable priority—finance eligibility, visual impact, or speed—changes the winner. Preserve negative findings even when they downgrade a favorite.

Use [the experiment guide](references/experiments.md) when designing validation or when a user asks for proof of demand, novelty, safety, or reliability. It includes distinctions that keep experiments honest. For reusable evaluation prompts and scoring criteria, read [the evaluation cases](evals/prompts.md).

Return a recommendation, its strongest objection, the smallest credible build, and the next observation that would change the decision. State exactly which demand or novelty claims remain unverified. Do not contact people, purchase, deploy, post, or move money merely to validate an idea unless the user authorized that action.
