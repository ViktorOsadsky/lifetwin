I would drop the claim that photo → appliance model → matching part → purchase is an original idea. Build a narrow shopping decision demo only if you are comfortable competing on execution: **a repair-cart audit that shows exactly why a part can be bought, or why the agent must stop**. With two builders and 30 hours, use one refrigerator family and removable bins/shelves. Keep purchase as an honest merchant handoff unless you obtain an authorized checkout integration.

The strongest objection is substantial: current products already cover the original flow and much of this narrower pivot. I did not find a defensible “nobody does this” claim in this research. This recommendation is a weekend build decision, not a claim of a new market.

There is a real problem, but demand for your proposed app is unvalidated. The [FTC's repair report announcement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2021/05/ftc-report-congress-examines-anti-competitive-repair-restrictions-recommends-ways-expand-consumers) identifies restrictions involving parts and diagnostic access. [PartSelect's help](https://www.partselect.com/help) documents the actual workaround: find the model, inspect diagrams, identify the component, and sometimes use serial/series distinctions. That supports the complexity of parts shopping. It does not establish how often your target shopper fails, what they would pay, or whether another assistant changes their buying decision. Existing paid tiers show an offer; they are not adoption/payment evidence for this app.

The promising inference is narrower: someone already considering a replacement part might value a compact decision record that separates appliance identity, part identity, fit evidence, selected offer and purchasing consent. That is worth testing as a concrete interaction, even though the broad capability is not novel.

I obtained real input before recommending the scope. A [public Whirlpool parts PDF](https://homedelivery.whirlpoolcorp.com/content/dam/global/documents/202212/repair-parts-list-w11645699-reva.pdf), revision dated 2022-11-08, explicitly covers WRF560SEHZ03 and three color variants. Its refrigerator-door list includes medium bin W10308869. The PDF is historical manufacturer evidence, not a current stock feed. A read-only GET to the XPart endpoint documented on its page, using `part=W10308869`, succeeded with one exact-identity result. The raw response is saved at `/private/tmp/calhacks-xpart-bin-lookup.json`.

That live response gives a useful demo complication: it reports out of stock, no current price, no direct cart URL, and only WRF560SEHZ00 in its returned verified-model list. This does **not** prove the part is incompatible with WRF560SEHZ03. It means the sources have different coverage and the offer is not purchase-ready. A good assistant should show the manufacturer's relationship, explain the limited catalog coverage, and decline to produce a buyable current offer from that result. The upstream confidence value is vendor metadata, not your measured correctness probability. The public endpoint has no reliability, rate-limit or reuse guarantee established in this test; save a timestamped replay and check terms before making it the sole critical dependency.

| Candidate | 30-hour gate | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General photo-to-repair-to-purchase agent | Broad coverage and merchant execution unavailable; direct competitors already present | No-go for an originality-led submission. |
| One-family evidence-aware repair-cart audit | Public manufacturer input was retrieved; read-only catalog fetch actually worked; merchant handoff needs no retailer credentials | Conditional go for a complete, memorable technical demo. Novelty remains weak/unproven. |
| Autonomous multi-retailer repair procurement | No retailer credentials or live transaction path; broad vendor/stock/return integration would dominate the build | No-go with this team/time. |

If visual polish and completion matter most, the narrow audit wins. If verified originality is the priority, none of these has passed that gate yet: spend a fixed first hour seeking a materially different purchasing workflow before committing. If a prize requires live financial execution, this project is blocked until access exists; a simulated payment does not satisfy that requirement by assumption.

The smallest credible slice is label photo → extracted exact identifier with visible confirmation → selected bin/shelf → manufacturer evidence card → current offer status → approved merchant handoff. Keep diagnosis out of this slice. Missing suffixes should ask for a clearer label rather than silently selecting a neighboring model. Do not infer incompatibility merely from absence in an incomplete catalog. Show the final merchant, exact part, quantity, currency and total when actually available. If price, selected variant, destination or merchant changes, require renewed approval. Do not claim an actual purchase or savings unless it happened.

Use the 30 hours deliberately: first 3 hours obtain a representative photo, one exact manufacturer relationship and one real offer lookup; kill or narrow scope if this fails. Then roughly 8 hours per builder for the UI/evidence path and backend/decision path in parallel, 6 hours integrating and handling failures, 4 hours on held-out evaluation, and 3 hours on the five-minute presentation and recovery recording. Those are elapsed team work blocks, not a promise of 60 hours of uninterrupted productivity.

The memorable branch is a near-identical label with a changed suffix. The agent should pause and show why its evidence no longer justifies the approved offer. The out-of-stock/no-price response is a second real-world branch. OCR and UI can be live if the team's model access works; manufacturer facts can be cached with revision/time; a catalog lookup can be live or replayed with its capture time; any payment/receipt without real access must be labelled simulated. A synthetic label photo is a synthetic OCR test, not a customer's appliance photograph.

Reserve 12 held-out cases: clear exact labels, ambiguous characters/missing suffix, a neighboring model, absent price, out of stock, stale offer, incomplete compatibility coverage, conflicting source and a material cart change. Have the second builder prepare them before tuning. Record exact-identifier accuracy, supported/unknown/blocked outcomes, citation correctness and final action. A sensible internal gate is zero unsupported checkout-ready decisions among these cases and a source link on every affirmative fit claim; it is not a statistical safety guarantee. No such evaluation has been run yet.

The next observation that would change my recommendation is an authorized test with three people who recently bought a replacement part: have each recreate their actual last purchase using their current workaround and your narrow prototype, then record errors, time, source trust and which they choose. Do not invent these interviews or contact people without authorization. Also try the same held-out tasks in the closest available competitors; a result they already handle equally well kills the proposed interaction advantage.

What remains unverified: willingness to adopt/pay, wrong-part frequency, the claimed interaction advantage, catalog coverage across real appliances, OCR robustness, API reuse terms and uptime, live merchant checkout, sponsor qualification and any category-wide originality. You can build a useful repair-shopping demo here; the current evidence does not justify selling it to judges as the first AI assistant to find compatible parts from a label photo.
